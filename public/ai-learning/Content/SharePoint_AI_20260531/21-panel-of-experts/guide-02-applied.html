What a council review actually returns

Point Review Council at a deck, a doc, or a PDF and the output has structure: each persona's short take, a combined verdict with a confidence score, and three working buckets — issues, strengths, and concrete fixes.

Each persona writes a short take

The first chunk of the output is one short paragraph per persona. The Adversary leads with its harshest read — on the small-business AI deck the demo ran, it snorts and says "this falls apart on the title promise." Every other persona follows with its own brief reaction: what works, what doesn't, framed from its lens.

The brevity is the point. Each persona delivers a punchy, opinionated take that's easy to read in seconds. You don't drown in commentary; you scan a panel of reactions.

Short individual takes beat one long combined essay. You can feel the spread between personas because they're sitting next to each other.

The combined verdict synthesizes the council

After the individual takes, the council attempts a combined view. On the small-business AI deck the verdict was: "You've got a conference session, not a usable content artifact about how AI will actually transform SMB." On the content-AI-management deck: "This deck wins nods in the room and loses force as a standalone file."

The combined verdict isn't a vote count or an average — it's a synthesized read that holds the tensions between personas. The Storyteller might love the hook the Architect thinks isn't load-bearing; the verdict names both and lands somewhere honest.

Synthesis is the value-add. Anyone can collect opinions. The council's job is to negotiate them into one defensible verdict.

Confidence is reported as a percentage

The verdict ships with a confidence score. In the demo, one verdict came in at 78% confidence. That number gives you a calibration handle — high confidence means the personas converged; lower confidence means they pulled in different directions.

Confidence isn't a grade on the content. It's a grade on the council's own conviction in its read. A 60% verdict still earns your attention; it just means there's genuine room to push back.

Confidence percentages aren't a quality score for your work. They're a self-report of how strongly the council backs its own verdict — useful for knowing where to argue.

Issues, strengths, and concrete fixes

The bottom of every review breaks into three buckets:

  • Issues — the things working against the content's intent.
  • Strengths — what's already pulling its weight; protect these.
  • Concrete fixes — specific edits to make, not vague suggestions.

The "concrete fixes" bucket is where the review stops being commentary and starts being a worklist. The demo's third example — an employee feedback transformation — even called out specific slides (PDF pages, really) that need rework. That's the artifact you can hand to a coauthor.

Three buckets make the review actionable. Issues say what to worry about; strengths say what not to break; concrete fixes say what to do tonight.
It's tempting to read only the issues. The strengths matter equally — they tell you what to protect when you revise so you don't fix one problem and create three.
