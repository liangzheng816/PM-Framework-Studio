How to Run FDE

Operational mechanics for turning field work into repeatable account growth and product improvement.

Objective: Understand team structure and execution loops required for reliable FDE delivery.

Echo and Delta role design

The transcript describes two core roles: Echo teams embedded with users and relationships, and Delta teams that ship software quickly under deployment pressure. The roles are complementary and intentionally asymmetric.

Echo protects problem quality; Delta protects solution velocity. Collapsing both into one profile usually weakens both functions.

Echo profiles combine domain fluency with challenger behavior, while Delta profiles emphasize rapid prototyping and tolerance for iterative rewrites. This division creates fast feedback without losing technical throughput.

FDE hiring is not generic "strong engineer" hiring. Role-specific traits drive performance more than raw technical pedigree.

Deployment cadence and expansion

A typical motion starts with one high-value use case, timeboxed execution, and leadership review. If that milestone lands, the team expands scope across adjacent problems in the same account.

Land-and-expand in FDE is not random upsell. It is staged expansion based on proven outcome credibility.

Execution quality depends on continuous account-level discovery after initial success. Teams should treat each newly opened workflow as both revenue opportunity and product learning opportunity.

Initial deployment success does not justify immediate broad rollout. Controlled expansion beats premature enterprise-wide promises.

Field-to-product translation loop

Field implementations produce tactical solutions first. Product teams then generalize those patterns into abstractions that can serve multiple customers and segments. This is the "gravel road to highway" transition.

Abstraction quality is the core compounding engine. Weak abstraction traps value in local custom code.

The transcript highlights involving FDEs from multiple accounts in design discussions to prevent overfitting product decisions to one account's local constraints.

Product teams cannot infer generalization correctly in isolation. Cross-account field evidence is required.

Demo-driven development as a control system

Repeated customer-facing demos force coherent end-to-end value, not just isolated feature quality. They expose integration friction early and align teams on user-desired workflows.

A strong demo is not presentation polish; it is proof that multiple capabilities connect into meaningful customer progress.

Demo loops also create an internal quality bar: if field teams repeatedly choose to use a product capability, that capability is likely delivering true leverage.

Demo-driven work is not superficial feature theater when it is tied to customer pain and measurable outcomes.
